The Mental Model — How GRC Actually Works

The single most important sentence in GRC

"Say what you do. Do what you say. Prove it."

Every GRC framework — 27001, 9001, 14001, 45001, SOX, NIST, PCI, HIPAA — fits in those nine words. If you forget every framework citation, remember this and you'll still be ahead of 80% of consultants.

  • "Say what you do" — policies, procedures, standards, the Statement of Applicability
  • "Do what you say" — operate the controls consistently, day in, day out
  • "Prove it" — generate evidence, keep records, allow it to be audited

SAY · DO · PROVE. When in doubt, ask "which of the three is Network18 missing on this topic?" You will find the answer instantly.

PART 3

ISO/IEC 27001:2022 — Deep Dive

What ISO 27001 actually is

ISO/IEC 27001 is an international standard for an Information Security Management System (ISMS). It is NOT a list of cyber controls. It is a way of managing cyber security systematically.

Two halves of the standard

HalfWhat it containsHow much it matters
Clauses 4-10 (Management System)The mandatory operating framework — context, leadership, planning, support, operation, evaluation, improvementThe spine. Failing here = failing the certification.
Annex A (Controls)93 controls in 4 themes. Pick what's applicable to you, document in SoA.The muscles. Most audit time spent here.
Anchor 3

"4-10 + 93/4"

ISO 27001:2022 = Clauses 4 to 10 (the management system) + 93 controls in 4 themes (Annex A). Memorize these two numbers and you can navigate the entire standard.

Left half = how you manage. Right half = what you do. Both must work together.

The 93 controls — broken down so you can talk to any of them

The Statement of Applicability (SoA) — your most important document

The SoA is a table listing all 93 controls, with three columns for each:

  1. Applicable? Yes/No
  2. If Yes — justification + implementation status + reference to control description
  3. If No — strong justification why it doesn't apply

SoA mistakes that fail audits:
  • Marking a control "Not Applicable" without justification
  • Justification is generic ("not relevant") rather than specific ("we don't operate industrial control systems")
  • "Applicable" but with no evidence of implementation
  • SoA not updated when scope or risk changes
  • SoA version not aligned to current ISMS Policy version
Anchor 5

The SoA is the audit's centre of gravity

External auditors spend more time on the SoA than any other document. Every "Yes" needs evidence of operation. Every "No" needs strong justification. Walk in knowing your SoA cold.

PART 4

Risk Methodology — How the ISMS Actually Thinks

The risk cycle — IATM

ISO 27001 Clause 6.1.2 requires a documented risk assessment methodology. The cycle has 4 stages.

Anchor 6

I-A-T-M

Identify · Assess · Treat · Monitor. Every information security risk goes through these four stages. If a risk is missing one of the four, the methodology is broken.

1. IDENTIFY — what could go wrong?

Two valid approaches (use both):

  • Asset-based: List assets (customer database, broadcast playout system, MoneyControl APIs). For each, list threats (ransomware, insider, vendor breach). For each threat-asset pair, that's a risk.
  • Scenario-based: List business-relevant scenarios ("CNBC TV18 goes off air for 2 hours due to cyber"), then work backwards to assets and threats.
Network18 indicative top-10 risks (Year 1 baseline)
  1. Ransomware encryption of broadcast playout systems → off-air event
  2. Insider exfiltration of pre-publication market-sensitive content (MoneyControl)
  3. Third-party SaaS breach exposing subscriber PII (DPDPA notification trigger)
  4. Compromise of journalist source-protection emails
  5. Defacement / unauthorized publishing on News18 platforms
  6. OTT/VOOT user-credential stuffing leading to account takeover
  7. Ad-tech partner breach affecting user tracking data
  8. Inability to comply with CERT-In 6-hour breach reporting
  9. DDoS during high-traffic event (election results, IPL, breaking news)
  10. SEBI material-event disclosure failure post-cyber incident

2. ASSESS — how big is the risk?

Score by Likelihood × Impact. Most enterprises use a 5x5 matrix. Network18 should pre-define scoring criteria so two analysts assess the same risk identically.

ScoreLikelihood criteriaImpact criteria (financial)
1 — RareLess than once in 10 years< ₹50 lakh
2 — UnlikelyOnce in 5-10 years₹50 lakh - ₹2 Cr
3 — PossibleOnce in 2-5 years₹2 Cr - ₹10 Cr
4 — LikelyOnce a year₹10 Cr - ₹50 Cr
5 — Almost CertainMultiple times per year> ₹50 Cr

3. TREAT — what do we do about it?

Four choices (5 if you count "Re-cost"):

  • Reduce (mitigate) — add controls. Most common.
  • Retain (accept) — knowingly keep the risk. Requires sign-off.
  • Refuse (avoid) — stop the activity that creates the risk.
  • Reassign (transfer) — insurance, contractual indemnity, outsourcing.
Anchor 7

4 R's of risk treatment

Reduce · Retain · Refuse · Reassign. Every risk on Network18's register must have a treatment chosen, documented, owned, and dated.

4. MONITOR — is it still true?

Risks change. Threats evolve. Treatments may stop working. Monitoring requires:

  • KRIs (Key Risk Indicators) attached to top risks — leading indicators
  • Quarterly risk register review (formal)
  • Trigger-based review (incident, regulation change, business change)
  • Management review at least annually (Clause 9.3)

Audit Finding Classifications

  • Major NC (Non-Conformity): a clause/control is not implemented or has serious systemic failure. Blocks certification.
  • Minor NC: isolated lapse in an otherwise functioning process. Does not block certification but must be addressed.
  • OFI (Opportunity for Improvement): works but could be better. Suggested, not mandatory.
The aim: Zero Major NCs going into external audit. Maximum 5-8 Minor NCs is normal and acceptable. If internal audit finds 0 issues, the audit was probably superficial.

Management Review Meeting (Clause 9.3) — what gets reviewed

Required at least annually. Network18 should run quarterly (better for the audit narrative).

  1. Status of actions from previous reviews
  2. Changes in external/internal issues (regulations, tech, business)
  3. ISMS performance: NCs, results of monitoring/measurement, audit findings, control effectiveness
  4. Feedback from interested parties (regulators, customers, board)
  5. Risk assessment results & status of risk treatment
  6. Opportunities for continual improvement

Minutes must record decisions made. Empty minutes = failed audit.

  • Mostly document review
  • 3-5 days typically

"Are you ready on paper?"

Stage 2 — Certification Audit

  • Sample evidence aggressively

Anchor 8

Stage 1 = paper. Stage 2 = practice.

Stage 1 audits your documents. Stage 2 audits whether you actually live by them. Both must pass.

What the Stage 2 auditor will physically do

  1. Opening meeting — confirm scope, timetable, expectations.
  2. Top management interview — verify leadership commitment (Clause 5).
  3. Document review on-site — SoA, risk register, policies, recent records.
  4. Control walkthroughs — pick 30-50 controls, walk through end-to-end with operators.
  5. Evidence sampling — "show me the last 5 access reviews", "show me 3 vendor reviews", "show me the last 4 incidents".
  6. Site visits — Mumbai HQ (full), Noida (sampled).
  7. Closing meeting — verbal findings, draft NC list.
  8. Report — within 2-4 weeks, certification recommendation.

Why integration works — the Annex SL "High-Level Structure"

All modern ISO management system standards share the same 10-clause structure. So Clauses 4-10 of each standard ask the same fundamental questions:

ClauseQuestionCommon answer across all 4
4. ContextWhat is the organization, who cares, what's in scope?Same scope statement for all 4 (Network18 + 2 locations)

8. OperationHow do we run it?Standard-specific operations (controls, processes)
9. PerformanceIs it working?One internal audit plan, one management review
10. ImprovementHow do we get better?One CAPA process
Each standard keeps its own subject-specific requirements but shares the core management system.

Benefits of integration — what to tell Network18 leadership

  • Audit cost reduction: 30-40% lower than four separate audits
  • Single management review instead of four

  • One document management system, consistent versioning

Mini Scenario

The "we'll integrate later" trap

A manufacturer pursued ISO 9001 in 2018 (built one system), ISO 14001 in 2020 (built another), ISO 45001 in 2022 (third), and finally ISO 27001 in 2024 (fourth).

They ended up with 4 separate policy frameworks, 4 risk registers, 4 internal audit teams (with overlapping skill needs), 4 management reviews per year. Total maintenance cost: ~₹2.8 Cr/year.

Integration in Year 5 took 18 months and cost ~₹1.5 Cr because they had to rewrite, retrain, and migrate.

Lesson: Build it integrated from day one. Network18 has the chance to do this right. We bring the architecture.

What happens when a vendor has an incident, fails a control, or changes ownership:

  • Decision: continue, escalate, terminate

Stage 5 — Offboard (When Engagement Ends)

The most-skipped stage. A vendor without offboarding is a former vendor with current access.

  • Access revocation checklist (Active Directory, SaaS apps, VPN, API keys, badges)
  • Data return or certified destruction
  • Contract close-out
  • Update vendor inventory
A simple decision tree. Run every vendor through it. Document the answer.

Five per year. These are deep, on-site (or deep remote) assessments of selected Tier-1 vendors. The output: a full report with risk rating, control findings, remediation roadmap, contractual requests.

Mini Scenario

The cloud shared-responsibility surprise

A retailer ran a TPRM assessment on AWS, treating it as a normal vendor review. The lead consultant asked AWS for evidence of "patching of operating systems in our environment." AWS replied: "That's your responsibility, not ours, under the shared responsibility model."

The retailer realized: a hundred controls they assumed AWS handled were actually theirs. They had no evidence those controls were operating. Six weeks of re-scoping followed.

Lesson: When assessing cloud vendors, map the shared responsibility model first. Then ask each side for evidence of their half. Don't assume.

Evidence — what auditors will ask for

Auditors don't accept verbal answers. They want artifacts. For every "Yes" in the SoA, you need evidence that the control is operating.

The 6 evidence types

  1. Policy or procedure document (proves it's defined)
  2. System configuration screenshot (proves it's technically enforced)
  3. Operational record — log, ticket, completed checklist (proves it runs)
  4. Approval / sign-off (proves authority chain)
  5. Training record / attestation (proves competence)
  6. Test result — internal audit, scan, BCP test (proves it works)
Anchor 10

Strong evidence = 3 of the 6

Weak evidence is one document. Strong evidence is 3+ types covering define / configure / operate. Walking into the audit, every Tier-1 control should have all 3.

Evidence quality test — apply to every artifact

TestQuestion
ProvenanceWho created this, when, in what system?
CompletenessIs this all of it, or curated?
IntegrityCould it have been altered? Is it timestamped?
TimelinessWas it captured when it happened, or retrofitted?
LinkageDoes it map cleanly to the specific control assertion?
The cardinal sin: Retrofitting evidence. Generating 12 months of "quarterly access reviews" the week before the audit is fraud — and good auditors spot it instantly (timestamps, file metadata, identical formatting). Better to admit a gap than fake the evidence.

The Q&A Bank — Questions You Will Be Asked

What Network18 CISO / CIO will ask in week 1

Q1: "Walk me through your approach in two minutes."
A: "We run a 4-phase approach across 12 months. Months 1-2 is a gap assessment — we'll do a structured review against ISO 27001:2022, ISO 9001/14001/45001, and your TPRM program at both Mumbai and Noida. Months 3-6 we build and remediate — integrated policy framework, risk register, SoA, and we close the priority gaps. Months 7-9 we run an internal audit and management review. Months 10-11 is the external Certification Body audit — Stage 1 then Stage 2. By month 12 you're certified across all four standards, with a TPRM program operating on 340 vendors and 5 deep external assessments done. We've built this on PDCA — the cycle every ISO standard uses."
Q2: "Why integrate the four standards? Sounds complicated."
A: "Because they share 70% of their structure — same Annex SL clauses 4-10. If you run them separately, you maintain four policies, four risk registers, four audit programmes, four management reviews. Integration saves you 30-40% on audit costs alone and avoids inconsistency. The unique part of each standard — Annex A controls for 27001, customer focus for 9001, environmental aspects for 14001, hazard ID for 45001 — sits on top of a shared core. Best time to integrate is at the start; we're at that moment."
Q3: "What's changed in ISO 27001:2022 vs 2013? We're on the old one."
A: "Three things to know. First, Annex A was restructured from 114 controls to 93, in 4 themes — Organizational, People, Physical, Technological. Second, 11 new controls were added — most importantly threat intelligence, cloud security, ICT readiness for BCM, data masking, information deletion, secure coding. Third, the clauses 4-10 are largely unchanged. If you were certified on 2013, you have to transition by 31 October 2025 — we're past that, so 2022 is the only option."
Q4: "How does TPRM scale to 340 vendors?"
A: "Risk-based tiering. We classify by data sensitivity, service criticality, network access, volume, and regulatory scope. Tier 1 — about 22 vendors — get full annual assessments and continuous monitoring. Tier 2 — 78 — get annual questionnaires plus sample evidence. Tier 3 — 240 — light-touch every 2 years. 80% of your risk sits in the top 22; 80% of your effort goes there. The 5 external deep assessments rotate across Tier 1 — typically cloud, ad-tech, CDN, critical SaaS, and one emerging-risk vendor."
Q5: "We use SOC 2 reports for most vendors. Isn't that enough?"
A: "SOC 2 is excellent when it covers the right scope. But the report's value is in what's inside the boundary — and most teams never check. We've seen a vendor's SOC 2 cover their HR systems while we were using their data analytics platform. Real assurance comes from: read the scope statement on page 3, map it to the service you actually buy, then test for gaps. We do this as part of every Tier-1 onboarding."
Q6: "What's the biggest risk to our certification?"
A: "Honestly, three patterns kill more certifications than anything else. First, the SoA — if it's stale or not aligned to operations, every audit conversation gets harder. Second, evidence discipline — having controls without artifacts is the same as not having them. Third, the management review meeting — if minutes are thin, the auditor concludes leadership isn't engaged. We invest heavily in those three from week 1."
Q7: "We're a media company. We have journalist sources to protect. How does ISO 27001 cover that?"
A: "Great question — it's specific to your sector. A.5.34 (privacy & PII protection) and A.8.11 (data masking) directly apply. Source protection becomes a Restricted information classification: encrypted communications, MFA-protected mailboxes, source identity stored separately from content, retention & deletion controls. We'll write a Source Protection sub-policy aligned to the ISMS and to your editorial guidelines. The integration with DPDPA 2023 also matters here."
Q8: "Will this slow down our editorial / business velocity?"
A: "Designed not to. The structural changes — policy framework, risk register, SoA, TPRM workflow — happen in parallel to operations, owned by your security and IT teams with our coaching. The places where there will be friction are at vendor onboarding for new Tier-1 and at access provisioning — but those should be slow on purpose. Editorial workflows we don't touch unless the gap report finds something specific."
Q9: "How does this work with DPDPA 2023?"
A: "DPDPA and ISO 27001 reinforce each other. ISO 27001 gives you the management system to operationalize DPDPA — consent management, retention & deletion, breach notification, data principal rights, DPIA. A.5.34, A.8.10 (information deletion) and A.8.11 (data masking) align directly. Our gap assessment will produce a DPDPA-mapped view: where the ISMS already covers DPDPA, where extra controls are needed."

Cheat Sheets & Mental Anchors

The 10 Anchors (memorize these)

  • 1. Trinity: Say · Do · Prove
  • 2. PDCA: Plan · Do · Check · Act = Q1 · Q2 · Q3 · Q4
  • 3. 4-10 + 93/4: Clauses 4-10 + 93 controls in 4 themes
  • 4. OPPT: Org 37 · People 8 · Physical 14 · Tech 34
  • 5. SoA: centre of gravity of the audit
  • 6. IATM: Identify · Assess · Treat · Monitor
  • 7. 4 R's: Reduce · Retain · Refuse · Reassign
  • 8. Stage 1 = paper; Stage 2 = practice
  • 9. 5 Os: Origin · Onboard · Operate · Oversee · Offboard
  • 10. Strong evidence = 3+ types covering define/configure/operate

What to say in any meeting

  • If you don't know: "Let me come back to you with a precise answer by [time]."
  • If pushed on cost: "I'll share the cost breakdown in the steering — happy to walk through after."
  • If pushed on timeline: "12 months is realistic for integrated cert with this scope. Faster is possible but risky."
  • If asked an inflammatory question: "Help me understand what you're really worried about."
  • If finding pushback: "The auditor will examine this evidence too. I'd rather we agree the right framing now."
  • If interrupted: Let them finish. Then: "To your point — [acknowledge] — and here's how it connects."

Phrases that destroy credibility (never say these)

  • "Per the framework..." → frameworks don't decide; people do
  • "It's a best practice." → too vague; cite the requirement specifically
  • "It depends." (without then explaining) → looks evasive
  • "In our experience..." → without an actual example
  • "That's outside scope." → too defensive; reframe as "I'll flag it and we'll decide together"
  • "The previous firm..." → never. Just don't.

Glossary — Speak Like You've Done This Before

ISMS

Information Security Management System — the documented, operating system Network18 uses to manage information security. ISO 27001 is the standard for the ISMS.

SoA (Statement of Applicability)

The document listing all 93 Annex A controls, marking each as Applicable / Not Applicable, with justification. The audit's centre of gravity.

CB (Certification Body)

The independent organization that audits and grants certification (BSI, DNV, BV, TUV, etc.). Must itself be accredited (e.g., by UKAS, NABCB).

Stage 1 / Stage 2 audit

Stage 1 = documentation review (paper-readiness). Stage 2 = implementation audit (practice-readiness). Both required for initial certification.

Surveillance audit

Smaller annual CB audit in Years 2 and 3 of the certification cycle. Confirms continued operation.

Recertification audit

Full re-audit at end of Year 3 to renew the certificate for another 3-year cycle.

NC (Non-Conformity)

Audit finding indicating a clause/control isn't met. Major = systemic / blocks certification. Minor = isolated / doesn't block but must be addressed.

OFI (Opportunity for Improvement)

Suggestion from the auditor — not mandatory. Tracking them shows continual improvement.

CAPA (Corrective & Preventive Action)

Process for closing audit findings and preventing recurrence. Required by Clause 10.

Annex A

The 93-control catalog in ISO 27001:2022. Organized as A.5 (Org), A.6 (People), A.7 (Physical), A.8 (Tech).

Annex SL / High-Level Structure (HLS)

The common 10-clause structure shared across all modern ISO management system standards. Makes integration possible.

Risk appetite vs tolerance vs capacity

Capacity = max you can absorb. Appetite = what board approves. Tolerance = comfortable operating range inside appetite.

Inherent vs Residual risk

Inherent = before controls. Residual = after controls. The difference is what your controls are buying.

TPRM

Third-Party Risk Management — the lifecycle program covering vendors / suppliers / partners.

SOC 2 (Type I vs Type II)

SOC 2 Type I = design of controls at a point in time. Type II = operating effectiveness over a period (typically 6-12 months). Type II is what you want.

Concentration risk

When too many critical services depend on one vendor. A failure at that vendor cascades. AWS / GCP outages are common examples.

Shared responsibility model

The cloud principle that vendor (AWS/GCP/Azure) is responsible for "security of the cloud" and customer is responsible for "security in the cloud." Many enterprises confuse this.

DPDPA 2023

Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 — India's personal data protection law. Drives consent, retention, deletion, breach notification, data principal rights.

CERT-In 6-hour rule

India's CERT-In directive (April 2022) requires reporting of specified cyber incidents within 6 hours of becoming aware. Among the strictest globally.

SEBI material event

Regulation 30 of SEBI LODR — listed companies must disclose "material events" within 30 minutes. A major cyber incident likely qualifies.

DPIA (Data Protection Impact Assessment)

Structured assessment for processing activities likely to result in high risk to data principals. Required under DPDPA for certain processing.

BCM / BCP / DR

BCM = Business Continuity Management (the overall discipline). BCP = the Plan. DR = Disaster Recovery (IT-specific subset).

RTO / RPO

Recovery Time Objective (how fast to restore) / Recovery Point Objective (how much data we can afford to lose).

Management Review (Clause 9.3)

Mandatory periodic top-management review of ISMS performance. Six required inputs. Minutes matter.

Internal Audit (Clause 9.2)

Mandatory independent audit of the ISMS against the standard and own requirements. Must occur before certification.

  2. Be the calmest person in the room. Especially during audit week.
